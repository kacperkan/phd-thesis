\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

With the advent of deep learning, researchers have explored various ways to
apply it to computer graphics.
One of the most recent and promising approaches is neural rendering, a field
that combines deep learning and computer graphics to generate realistic images
of 3D scenes.
A prominent neural rendering technique is the neural radiance field
(NeRF~\cite{mildenhall2020nerf}), which represents a 3D scene as a continuous
function mapping 3D coordinates to radiance values.
NeRF has demonstrated impressive results in synthesizing photorealistic images
but suffers from high memory and computational demands, making it challenging
to scale to large scenes.
Moreover, its implicit representation prohibits its use in real-time
applications such as game engines.

To address these limitations, \textcite{kerbl20233d} introduced 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS), a neural rendering technique that represents a 3D scene as a
set of 3D Gaussians, which are splatted into image space using the algorithm
proposed by~\textcite{zwicker2001ewa}.
Unlike NeRF, 3DGS is more memory efficient and can render large scenes in real
time.
It enables rendering millions of points at interactive frame rates on a single
GPU, making it a viable option for real-time applications.

This thesis addresses the challenges of achieving controllability in neural
rendering, with a focus on Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) and Gaussian
Splatting.
The work presented here reflects my research journey as I tackled key
obstacles in controlling texture, expression, lighting, and computational
efficiency in radiance fields.
In the following sections, we describe the motivation behind this work,
outline the research questions that guide our inquiry, summarize my
contributions, and finally, provide an overview of the thesis structure.

\section{Motivation and Challenges}

  NeRF and 3DGS are powerful techniques for generating realistic images.
  However, once trained, their representations become static and unmodifiable.
  This lack of flexibility makes it challenging to control key properties such
  as lighting, subject attributes, and scene composition, which are crucial for
  practical applications in graphics and vision.
  Enabling controllability in these models is essential to expanding their
  usability beyond passive scene reconstruction.
  This thesis aims to address this challenge by exploring methods to introduce
  intuitive and efficient control mechanisms.

  Controllability is a fundamental requirement in computer graphics.
  Our inspiration comes from traditional artistic workflows, where iterative
  refinement is key.
  Consider a game artist designing a 3D character: they carefully model the
  mesh, generate a UV map, and apply textures.
  If their supervisor requests changes, modifying the design without disrupting
  the existing structure is crucial.
  In contrast, current neural rendering methods lack this flexibility—altering a
  trained NeRF or 3DGS representation often requires extensive retraining.
  This limitation makes real-time edits impractical and hinders their adoption
  in interactive applications.

  Addressing this issue requires novel approaches that integrate controllability
  without compromising rendering quality or efficiency.
  In this thesis, we explore techniques that bridge the gap between neural
  representations and the adaptability demanded by real-world applications.

  \paragraph{Data-driven controllability.}
    Since the introduction of NeRFs~\cite{mildenhall2020nerf},
    NeRFies~\cite{park2021nerfies} pioneered high-quality reconstructions from
    casual video sequences of dynamic scenes.
    However, NeRFies initially provided only limited control, relying on simple
    linear interpolation of latent embeddings to represent time.

    HyperNeRF~\cite{park2021hypernerf} addressed this limitation by introducing a
    high-dimensional embedding space, which is projected onto a lower-dimensional
    representation to enable more interpretable scene traversal.
    Despite this improvement, the projection remains non-linear, making the
    effects of adjustments unpredictable and difficult to control.

    To overcome these challenges, we propose CoNeRF~\cite{kania2022conerf}, which
    leverages the smoothness of Multilayer Perceptrons
    (MLPs)~\cite{tancik2020fourier,park2021nerfies} to introduce controllability
    through sparse supervision.
    Our approach trains a weakly-supervised MLP using at least 100 frames, with
    only a subset receiving coarse annotations that define attribute values and
    their spatial influence.
    Published at CVPR 2022, our method enables flexible, interpretable edits to
    NeRFs while maintaining high rendering quality.

  \paragraph{Template-based controllability.}
    Methods such as EditNeRF~\cite{liu2021editing} and FigNeRF~\cite{xie2021fig}
    focus on static elements, restricting control to colors and textures.
    Additionally, our initial experiments revealed that
    CoNeRF~\cite{kania2022conerf} struggles with global deformations, such as a
    person performing jumping jacks.
    While data-driven approaches like~\textcite{fang2022fast} use multi-scale
    voxel structures to model deformations, these methods perform well primarily
    in synthetic settings~\cite{pumarola2021d}.

    An alternative strategy relies on template-based models, where a 3D mesh is
    used to canonicalize deformed points~\cite{zielonka2022instant}.
    However, these methods are highly sensitive to registration accuracy, which is
    inherently imperfect~\cite{feng2021learning, zielonka2022towards}.
    Even minor misalignments are amplified in radiance field models, which assume
    a perfectly calibrated scene.
    To compensate, existing approaches introduce latent
    spaces~\cite{grassal2022neural, kania2022conerf, martin2021nerfw} that require
    thousands of frames to capture high-fidelity deformations, making them
    impractical for data-efficient learning.

    To address these challenges, we propose \blendfields, a data-efficient method
    inspired by traditional blendshapes~\cite{lewis2014practice}.
    Building on VolTeMorph's~\cite{garbin2024voltemorph} approach to point
    canonicalization, BlendFields introduces a physically-based mixture of
    predefined, data-driven wrinkle templates that capture expression-dependent
    skin deformations.
    This method significantly reduces data requirements while maintaining high
    fidelity, making it a practical solution for real-world applications.
    Our work on BlendFields was accepted at CVPR 2023.

  \paragraph{Physically-based illumination controllability.}
    Beyond controlling shape and texture, realistic scene illumination is
    essential for practical applications.
    However, inverse rendering is inherently ill-posed, as multiple lighting
    conditions can produce identical observations~\cite{patow2003survey}.
    Some approaches rely on datasets of single objects under diverse lighting
    conditions~\cite{chen2022hallucinated,yang2023crnerf,rudnev2022nerfosr}.
    These methods either fail to disentangle albedo from
    lighting~\cite{chen2022hallucinated, yang2023crnerf} or require auxiliary
    networks to predict shadows~\cite{rudnev2022nerfosr}, thereby limiting their
    practicality.

    We propose \lumigauss, which builds on the high-quality surface reconstruction
    capabilities of 2D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{huang20242d}.
    By integrating precomputed radiance transfer techniques~\cite{slomp2006gentle,
      ramamoorthi2001envmap}, \lumigauss achieves high-fidelity rendering with
    realistic, physics-based lighting.
    This work was accepted at WACV 2025.

  \paragraph{Efficiency controllability.}
    Volumetric representations are computationally expensive compared to
    traditional meshes.
    Training a NeRF model on a single scene can take several days on an NVIDIA
    V100 GPU, with rendering times exceeding 60 seconds per image---far from
    practical applications.
    While various speed-up methods
    exist~\cite{garbin2021fastnerf,yu2021plenoctrees,reiser2021kilonerf,hedman2021baking,
      mueller2022instant}, they often trade off memory, quality, or speed.

    3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\cite{kerbl20233d} has emerged as a powerful
    alternative, offering high-quality rendering at interactive frame rates.
    However, achieving these speeds still requires high-end GPUs.
    We see promise in 3DGS as a canonical 3D representation, similar to meshes,
    yet its adaptability across different computational resources remains an open
    challenge.
    Traditional meshes employ level-of-detail (LoD) techniques to reduce detail
    where needed without compromising overall perception.
    We seek to develop analogous strategies for 3DGS to enable flexible,
    resource-aware rendering.

    Therefore, we propose \clog~\cite{kania2024clog}, a method that predicts the
    target Gaussian Splatting representation for a specified LoD.
    \clog learns the
    underlying representation once and dynamically adapts it in a feed-forward
    manner.
    Our work is currently under review at CVPR 2025.

\section{Research Objectives}
  In this thesis, we explore diverse avenues to achieve radiance field
  controllability.
  Our goal is to answer the following research questions:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\textbf{RQ \arabic*})]
    \item \label{enum:texturing}
          Can a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) be endowed with controllability by
          leveraging sparse annotations in the training dataset?
          How many annotations are sufficient to enable smooth interpolation between
          controlled values?
    \item \label{enum:movement}
          Are extreme facial expressions, as documented in the literature, adequate for
          learning expression-dependent details that extrapolate to unseen expressions
          during training?
    \item \label{enum:lighting}
          Is it possible to learn an underlying radiance transfer function of a scene
          from images captured in unconstrained, ``in-the-wild'' settings?
          Can this transfer function generalize to novel environment maps?
    \item \label{enum:resources}
          How can we learn a single 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation that is
          adaptable to different computational regimes at inference time in a
          feed-forward manner?
  \end{enumerate}

  Each question represents one of many possible directions for achieving
  controllability in radiance fields.
  In this thesis, we categorize our methods according to four control aspects:
  texture~\cite{kania2022conerf,kania2023blendfields},
  shape/expression~\cite{kania2023blendfields},
  lighting~\cite{kaleta2024lumigauss}, and resource usage~\cite{kania2024clog}.

  \subsection{Texture from Partial Information}
    To address \ref{enum:texturing}, we introduce \conerf~\cite{kania2022conerf},
    a pioneering approach that leverages sparse annotations to control subject
    attributes in a post-hoc manner.
    Early NeRF-based methods~\cite{liu2021editing} were simple models—overfit to a
    single subject and requiring retraining for new subjects—with limited
    editability.
    More sophisticated models, such as NeRFactor~\cite{zhang2021nerfactor},
    operated only on simple, calibrated scenes and could not handle motion.
    Inspired by HyperNeRF~\cite{park2021hypernerf}, \conerf revisits weak
    supervision in radiance fields.
    By assuming that only a few sparse annotations (including region-specific
    labels) are provided, and by exploiting the smoothness of the MLPs used in
    NeRFs~\cite{tancik2020fourier}, the annotation signal is propagated across
    similar frames.
    This approach not only enables semantic segmentation of radiance fields but
    also decouples attribute controls.
    Such coarse annotations that can be generated in a few minutes suffice to
    achieve both novel view synthesis and novel attribute manipulations, though
    the annotation complexity grows with the number of controlled attributes.

  \subsection{Expression from Few-Shot Learning}
    In response to \ref{enum:movement}, we propose
    \blendfields~\cite{kania2023blendfields}, which extends \conerf to handle
    dynamic facial expressions.
    Although \conerf is capable of rendering motions given sufficient data and
    annotations, acquiring such data for complex motions (\eg, speaking full
    sentences) is challenging.
    Prior methods like VolTeMorph~\cite{garbin2024voltemorph} employ face template
    models (such as FLAME~\cite{li2017flame}) to derive a canonical representation
    by using a tetrahedral cage that moves points to a fixed ``neutral'' position.
    However, this procedure is insufficient to capture realistic facial features
    like wrinkles.
    Our novel contribution, \blendfields, builds on VolTeMorph by computing a
    deformation gradient for each tetrahedron—a physically based, interpretable
    quantity that guides smooth transitions in facial textures.
    Each NeRF branch is overfit to a specific extreme expression, and the model
    predicts face bases conditioned on the expression vector to output the final
    color.
    This framework yields spatially coherent, expression-dependent details even
    when provided with only a few extreme expressions.

  \subsection{Light from Unconstrained Images}
    For \ref{enum:lighting}, we introduce \lumigauss~\cite{kaleta2024lumigauss} to
    tackle the challenge of illumination controllability.
    Previous approaches assumed idealized capture conditions with constant camera
    exposure and lighting, resulting in blended colors that preclude dynamic light
    changes.
    In contrast, our approach decouples the intrinsic color of the subject from
    lighting effects by learning the radiance transfer function directly from
    unconstrained photo collections.
    We employ 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS)~\cite{huang20242d} to obtain a smooth
    and spatially coherent surface—a task that is difficult with
    3DGS~\cite{kerbl20233d}.
    By endowing the Gaussians with additional features corresponding to radiance
    transfer, expressed as Spherical Harmonics~\cite{green2003grittydetail},
    \lumigauss renders realistic scenes and shadows that adapt to novel
    environment maps, achieving higher fidelity than prior approaches.

  \subsection{Levels of Detail in One Model}
    Finally, to address \ref{enum:resources}, we propose
    \clog~\cite{kania2024clog} as a solution to the high computational demands of
    current radiance field models.
    All the contributions above typically require training dedicated models on
    high-end hardware.
    Inspired by the gaming industry’s Levels of Detail (LoD) techniques, CLoG
    trains a single Gaussian Splatting model that can be modulated at inference
    time to adapt to various computational regimes with minimal loss in rendering
    quality.
    By constraining the number of Gaussians in 3DGS~\cite{kerbl20233d} to a fixed
    count—reshaped into a 2D grid—and using a coarse-to-fine training protocol,
    the model learns a high-quality volumetric representation.
    In a subsequent training stage, we spatially sort the Gaussians so that
    similar descriptors are adjacent, forming a low-frequency image that is
    upsampled using an off-the-shelf continuous
    architecture~\cite{vasconcelos2023cuf}.
    Our experiments demonstrate that even with as few as 2,000 Gaussians (compared
    to the typical $10^5$--$10^6$), the object remains recognizable and the model
    adapts continuously—eliminating the need to retrain for different LoD
    settings.

    The integrated approach above addresses texture, expression, lighting, and
    resource adaptability, offering a comprehensive solution to radiance field
    controllability.

\section{Contributions}
  Based on these research questions, my contributions are summarized in four
  major projects, each corresponding to a key control dimension in radiance
  fields.

  \paragraph{CoNeRF: Controllable Neural Radiance Fields.}
    I introduced the idea of using partial annotations as a control signal for
    learning controllable radiance fields.
    For this work, I developed the entire Python codebase, designed the final
    \conerf architecture, created all the datasets, conducted the experiments, and
    wrote the initial version of the article.
    This work laid the foundation for controlling subject attributes post-hoc and
    motivated further exploration into handling larger deformations.

  \paragraph{BlendFields: Few-Shot Example-Driven Facial Modeling.}
    To overcome \conerf's limitations in modeling large deformations, I extended
    the approach in \blendfields---a method currently included in a US patent
    submission.
    I designed and performed early experiments that exposed the shortcomings of
    existing condition-based approaches for realistic facial wrinkle modeling in a
    few-shot setting.
    Motivated by discussions with my collaborators, I explored the use of
    wrinkle-maps within neural radiance fields, devised the branched architecture,
    and executed the experimental evaluations.
    I also authored the initial version of the paper and contributed substantially
    to its refinement.

  \paragraph{LumiGauss: High-Fidelity Outdoor Relighting with 2D Gaussian Splatting.}
    For \lumigauss, which addresses the challenge of illumination controllability,
    I played a supervisory role.
    Although I was not the first author on this project, I contributed to the
    design of the final architecture, authored the revised version of the paper,
    corrected figures, and oversaw further improvements.
    This work decouples intrinsic subject color from environmental lighting by
    learning the radiance transfer function from unconstrained images.

  \paragraph{CLoG: Leveraging UV Space for Continuous Levels of Detail.}
    I developed a solution for adapting a single 3D Gaussian Splatting model to
    varying computational regimes.
    In \clog, I designed and performed the initial experiments that identified the
    primary research goal, built the complete architecture and training regime,
    implemented the full codebase, executed all experiments, and wrote the initial
    version of the article.
    This method enables continuous adaptation to different levels of detail in a
    feed-forward manner, eliminating the need for retraining when hardware
    constraints change.

    Collectively, these contributions explore complementary strategies for
    achieving radiance field controllability.
    They not only address distinct control dimensions—texture, expression,
    lighting, and resource efficiency—but also interrelate to form a cohesive
    framework for advancing neural rendering.

\section{Thesis Outline}
  The thesis is organized into four main chapters, each corresponding to one of
  the research questions and contributions described above.
  In \cref{chap:conerf}, we introduce CoNeRF, our approach for controlling
  neural radiance fields using partial annotations.
  Next, \cref{chap:blendfields} presents BlendFields, a method that generates
  realistic, expression-dependent textures from just a few multi-view frames.
  \cref{chap:lumigauss} details LumiGauss, a Gaussian
  Splatting model that learns a radiance transfer function to enable novel lighting
  effects in unconstrained settings.
  Finally, \cref{chap:clog} describes CLoG, a general approach that leverages
  Gaussian Splatting to achieve continuous levels of detail across different
  computational regimes.
  We conclude in \cref{chap:final} with a discussion of future research
  directions and the broader impact of this work.
